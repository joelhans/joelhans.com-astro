Of all the ideas I've stood for and implemented at ngrok, by building brand
through developer education, the closest to my heart is that _you must give more
than you take_.

To do that, you must offer people understanding and curiosities they can walk
away with that goes far beyond your product.

Getting that right, not just once, but for the long haul, is extraordinarily
difficult.

## What a little internet has taught me

I've been working for a few months on a project called the [little
internet](https://github.com/ngrok/little-internet). It's a physical network,
using Raspberry Pis and Ethernet cables, that will someday behave exactly like
the real internet, but... little.

So far, I've learned about `tshark`, ARP, DHCP, dnsmasq, and more, but that's
not the point.

The point is to watch these systems intricately connect, despite having separate
concerns, to work in near-instantaneous concert. The joy in seeing into, and
then much more deeply understanding, the shadows between the systems I have
always taken for granted.

Because I've looked into those shadows, I can now do things I didn't even
realize were possible before. I can log onto the Wi-Fi at the coffee shop, turn
on `tshark` filtered for ARP frames, and watch dozens of devices persistently
"shouting" to the network about where they live and what other devices they're
looking for.

Now, when I create content about this project, it's primarily to share this joy
of discovery through experience, and give you an understanding of these systems
along the way, so that you too could experience networking in entirely new ways.

That's the whole idea.

Someone ought to read or watch something we make and find new understanding of
how processes relate to one another. They ought to know how to make better, more
independent decisions about the things they build because of it.

## How that works day to day

We're working on a video about n8n and ngrok together. There's a very natural
path to creating a video that starts with installing n8n, followed by ngrok,
wiring them up, and moving on.

That may be helpful to some, and isn't necessarily giving more than we take, but
it runs close. We aim for better.

We can ask pointed questions about the parts where we take:

- How can we describe and empathize with their problems in ways that are not framed around our product? 
- How much of ngrok can we excise and still maintain a logical story that follows a beginning, middle, and end? 
- What does the viewer learn, and can meaningfully use, even if they stop watching just before ngrok shows up?

And we bolster those with durable learning about the systems and processes at
the heart of the problem they're trying to solve. For example, we could give you
a handful of `docker` commands that you copy-paste into your setup, _or_ we
could give you an entire repo with a `docker-compose.yaml` file and
production-grade defaults baked in. We could teach you why having a separate
database service matters. We could even explain what `localhost` is and why,
jokes aside, you can't share `http://localhost:5678`.

That's giving more than we take.

We give them the best possible instruction on how to make a stable, secure, and
hardened public n8n instance. We hope they'll use ngrok to make it accessible to
the world.

It's more important they become a power user of solving their problem than a
champion of ngrok.

## A fun (but hard) bar to jump every day

Even in a world where I try to give more than I take, I do want people to know
ngrok is an option, to understand that we put the same care into our products
that we put into our content, and try us.

But I want to bring them to that place by letting them linger in, and then hold
onto, all of that systems thinking, even their choices never lead them to us. We
are still doing the right work in the right way.

It's hard to get right.

Leadership often looks to content teams for pipeline and revenue. It's tempting
to believe the audience needs you more than you need them—that you're the one
who holds the cards. It's easy to think you can "sneak out" a few low-quality,
extracitive pieces designed for agents to consume... because _agents aren't
people, right?_. But these kinds of questions impose an almost-invisible tax on
how people perceive what you do and the products you build.

And I'm willing to admit this generosity play only truly works when your
priority is awareness and shifting perception, not generation of demand.

But when you're given the opportunity to try to capture attention with your
genuine curiosity, care, and willing to learn, and _maybe_ then earning their
attention when you do have product to discuss? 

Then you can help people see into the shadows between the systems all around
them. Take joy in the process of discovery. Give people new keeness to build
their own systems with newfound confidence.

Then you can give it all away.
